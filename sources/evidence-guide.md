# How to Read the Evidence

[Book home](../README.md) · [Contents](../CONTENTS.md) · [Bibliography](bibliography.md)

An encyclopedia entry can combine several kinds of statement: a physical
principle, a reported observation, an interpretation, and an imagined system.
The status at the top is a starting point. Each substantial claim still needs
its own scope and source.

## Status and Scope

| Label | Use it when… | Do not infer… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Established | The stated principle or capability has a well-supported basis. | Every application of it will work. |
| Research | A relevant investigation is cited and its actual result is described. | Routine availability, general effectiveness, or a complete product. |
| Proposed | The entry describes a design without documenting a working implementation. | That a prototype has been built or evaluated. |
| Speculative | Essential mechanisms, addressing, integration, or perceptual outcomes remain unresolved. | That familiar components establish the proposed combination. |
| Frontier | The entry explores a possibility with major unresolved scientific premises. | Demonstrated capability or an inevitable future development. |

These categories are editorial aids, not a linear maturity scale. A page may
need a mixed description, such as “research on stimulation; speculative as a
scene display.” A laboratory effect, a simulation, and a practical aid answer
different questions.

Earlier drafts used **Prototype** for plausible design concepts. Entries
without a documented implementation now use **Proposed**. When an actual
prototype is cited, describe what was built, where it was tested, and what it
did; the word alone is not evidence.

## Reading Across Levels

Suppose a source demonstrates a conversion from magnetic energy to a local
electrical effect. That can support a claim about the conversion. A proposed
visual interface still needs an addressable output, a perceptual result, and
evidence that a user can interpret it. The missing steps should be written
beside the proposal rather than hidden by the citation.

Use this sequence when reading an entry:

1. Identify the claim: a mechanism, a sensation, recognition, task performance,
   learning, or practical usefulness.
2. Identify the evidence: device bench test, simulation, animal study, human
   study, review, historical document, or conceptual proposal.
3. Identify the scope: which population, device, task, setting, and date.
4. Ask what was measured and what comparison was used.
5. Ask which part of the entry is the author's extension beyond that result.

An effect in a particular study is worth documenting even when its eventual
application is uncertain. A negative result can also strengthen an entry by
showing which assumption needs to change.

## Writing a Stronger Entry

Place a short evidence paragraph after the chain map. Name the source, year,
study type, observed result, and its boundary. Put proposed extensions in a
separate section and describe what evidence would strengthen or weaken them.

Keep **intended output** separate from **reported percept**. Keep memory
vividness separate from memory accuracy, and a neural measurement separate
from what a participant recognized. Use “not established by this source” when
the limitation is the source's scope; absence from one paper is not proof of
universal impossibility.
